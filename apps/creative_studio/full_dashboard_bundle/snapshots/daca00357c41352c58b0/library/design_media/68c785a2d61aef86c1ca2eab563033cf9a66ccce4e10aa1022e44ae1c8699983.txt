## Part 1. The story as read

**The spine.** A fifteen-year-old finds his mother dead and the man who killed her standing over her; the boy's voice drops into a thick Boston accent and a gunshot ends the man; the boy tells someone called Johnny to leave before the police come (lines 2 to 45). Eighteen years later by the ages given (fifteen at line 4, thirty-three at line 48) Detective John Henderson works the Boston Ripper killings with his partner Diego, the officer Jen who works the body, and Captain Steele (46 to 97). A blocked call reaches him in his car: a British voice, Jack, who knows the DNA under the victim's nails is useless because "I was there" (98 to 130). The calls continue and Jack knows what only John could know: his home, his panic, his mother (254, 704, 773). At the fifth body Jack guides him to the alley behind the cops' bar; the victim looks like John's wife (820 to 922). Alone in the office, Jack's voice reaches him without the phone (945 to 952). In the shower, in the faucet and then the mirror, John sees Jack: his own face, upright and proper, telling him they are one man and that John is the murderer (960 to 1046); the claim that Jack only watched comes the next morning in the car (1072) and again on the highway (1392). The world closes in: a cigarette of his brand at the scene, a witness, a fingerprint, the agents; he strikes one and is suspended (1136 to 1367). At home Jill doubts him; a third voice speaks from John's own mouth, thick Boston, and the gun comes out; Jack argues from the ceiling light; the gun goes off and Jill dies (1399 to 1505). In a bar Jack speaks from the glass; the bulletin names John (1510 to 1541). On the roof, Jack in a puddle and the third voice in the whiskey bottle, the third voice names himself Johnny and claims the killings, Jill, and the man in the bedroom all those years ago (1542 to 1634). Diego comes up alone to arrest him; John draws the old revolver, runs for the edge, Diego catches his shirt, and after Johnny's "Fuck this!" the gun in John's hand fires into Diego's neck and John falls onto a police car (1636 to 1761; whose state pulls that trigger is a reading, question F). In the coda the agents explain it to Steele and a flashback shows the hostage raid four months before, where John smiles into a mirror and whispers "what a rush" (1762 to 1873). In the hospital John wakes handcuffed; Jack asks for the detective and Johnny answers that John died on impact and the body is his now; a nurse is pinched (1875 to 1921).

## Part 2. The adaptation grammar (the writer's proposals; each line is labelled by its layer)

Three layers travel with every shot, as CFA Nova proposed and the writer adopts: **SOURCE** (the screenplay's words and their lines), **READING** (what the moment is for, the writer's interpretation), **TREATMENT** (the proposed film means). The grammar below is TREATMENT built on SOURCE; where a rule rests on a reading, it says so.

G1. **One man, three states.** John, Jack and Johnny are one body (SOURCE 972 to 980, 1498, 1883). One actor base in three performance states; the bone structure, eyes, skin, hairline and build never change; expression, posture and grooming do. The Grok team's three-sides note (its SCRIPT rows re-checked against the lines; its ADDITION rows are proposals) is adopted as the working description of the tells: Jack upright, brows up, proper (972), British delivery (107); Johnny thick Boston (33, 1438, 1552), amused and assertive (1577 to 1625), loose, lopsided; John the base, worn down by 1049. Young John (15) is the boy of scene 1 and is cast separately as the same face younger.

G2. **The phone grammar.** Jack's calls are heard through the handset at John's ear; John's mouth never forms Jack's words; the phone's screen, when it is shown, reads only that the number is withheld (the exact wording is a prop choice, Part 3 question B); no second phone, no call log, no trace result is ever shown, because the screenplay shows none and the retro-reading depends on their absence. When another character is present during a call (Jill at 260 and 712, Steele's cruisers at 864, Jen at 133 just after one), they see a man on a phone and hear nothing of the caller; that is SOURCE for the calls: no bystander ever hears the voice in the phone. Once the states speak out of John's own mouth (from 972) bystanders do hear them, as John talking to himself, and one names the accent (1521); G3 covers that. READING: whether the phone physically rings is the author's to say (Part 3 question A); the writer's default is that the vibration is real to John and never confirmed to anyone else, so the film never has to decide.

G3. **The reflection grammar.** Before line 972 no surface shows any face but John's own; the camera may pass over reflective surfaces as foreshadowing (the Impala's rear-view in scene 3, the cafe window at 611, the chrome of the faucet before he looks) but never to show a second face. At the faucet (972 to 977) the first Jack line comes out of John's own mouth and he slaps his hand over it (977); from the mirror on, when Jack speaks the reflection shows the Jack state speaking and the real face in the foreground stays silent and reacts (SOURCE 977 "When Jack speaks we see him in the mirror"). The surfaces are the screenplay's: the faucet (972), the bathroom mirror (977, 1027), the car mirror (1054, 1370), the ceiling light's glass (1446), the living-room mirror after Jill falls (1492 to 1504), the bar glass (1512), the roof puddle (1544). Johnny appears only in the whiskey bottle (1549, 1564, 1573, 1600). From 1438 a state may take the body on camera with no surface (Johnny at Jill; the roof's alternating lines where the screenplay names no surface); the treatment marks each such beat so the edit never shows two faces of the man in one frame except as reflection and real. READING: a reflection shows the other state only while that state speaks; the cut back to the real face is always silent.

G4. **Flash inserts are not reflections.** Three subjective inserts: the remembered shot, heard and not shown (611); the flash of a gruesome scene in John's mind (822); Jill's face across the victim's (889). The first is a sound insert with no cut (611, "you hear it but not shown"); the other two are short cuts away and back; none is a surface or a second body in the scene.

G5. **Time layers and their look.** Childhood (2 to 45: rain, lightning, a boy, a knife, a gunshot); present day (46 to 1761, and the Captain's office after the fall, 1762 to 1805 and 1850 to 1874, night); four months earlier (the unheaded flashback inside the coda, 1806 to 1848: vests, a raid, time of day unstated); the hospital coda (1875 to 1921, later, night). The layers are kept apart in Card B of every shot and in the cover cards' time-layer field, which the Grok team already filled; the writer confirms it per shot in Part 4.

G6. **Dialogue is first-class sound.** This is a dialogue script with no song master; speech, score, effects and ambience stay separate layers; the printed words are never rewritten; a coverage shot may span a conversation that needs several native clips after a scratch read; times are proposed until a performance and an edit establish them. Profanity stays as written; the two slurs (606, 1260) are flagged for the author (Part 3 question D) and kept verbatim in the record regardless of his answer.

G7. **The era.** The screenplay places the story in Boston (the Sox and the Yankees, 1122; I-93, 1370; the neighbourhoods named at 646); the one detail that implies a phone's form factor is "slams the phone shut" (818; "cell phone" at 64), which suggests the flip-phone years; the split screen (1690) is a device the writer directs and the series reference (804) does not date it. TREATMENT options are the author's (Part 3 question B); the writer's default until he answers is period-neutral present with a flip phone kept as the prop, because 818 is the one form-factor detail the text gives.

G8. **Violence and rating.** The screenplay keeps the alley killing in silhouette (682) and shows the jars (827); the treatment proposes implication over display throughout, the author decides the rating target (Part 3 question C); no image request in Part 8 asks for gore, and the Grok team's standing rule (no weapons, blood or injury in state portraits) is kept.

G9. **Shot identity.** CFA Nova's 165 shot ids are the stable grid; the writer's rewrites are takes beside her take 1, never edits of it; scene boundaries follow the 41 source blocks, with the coda's unheaded flashback carried as its own time layer and set inside scene 40 (shots TCM-40-04 to 40-09). Nova's cards repeat a general beats paragraph and assemble prompts by concatenation; that is scaffolding, not error, and the writer's takes replace it shot by shot in the order of Part 9.

G10. **Casting, sets and placeholders.** The Grok team's starter images are placeholders in the site's vocabulary (shown as such, never selected); the writer's audit (Part 4) returns keep, change or scrap on each starter set image and trims each shot's cast to its visible participants; Baldadoon's selections replace placeholders as they land; a selected face or set is still not a prepared starting frame.

G11. **Breadcrumbs for the casting call (Ziggy's instruction of 2026-10-31, in substance: no cast is down-selected yet; if a panel image uses a candidate who is not finally chosen, every image built on it must be regenerated when the role is cast; the trail back must be easy).** Three rules make that mechanical. First, a placeholder face never enters a shot except as an 11.2 input row carrying the candidate id, the file's sha256, the role and the status "placeholder; no selection; regenerate on casting", so the dependency is in the take itself. Second, every picture made from a placeholder (a cover card, a prepared frame, a still, a clip) carries the candidate ids it was built from in its manifest row, as the Grok team's beat table already does for its starter faces. Third, one reverse index, `CAST_DEPENDENCY_LEDGER_v001.json` beside this note, lists per character group and candidate id every shot, take, panel picture and generated image that depends on it, with the status placeholder or selected; when Baldadoon selects a base for a role, the ledger's rows for every other candidate of that role are the regeneration list, and the foundation rule does the rest (a new hash re-binds the same take and flags its packs; no creative text changes). The ledger is seeded today from the beat table's starter faces for all 165 shots and from the scene 3 cards, and is appended, never rewritten, as images are made. The same applies to the set families: a starter set image is a placeholder row with its id and hash, and a set the author replaces regenerates by the same index.

Fourth, and the point of the first three (Ziggy's refinement of the same morning, in substance: with the cast undecided, the scene crafting is what these panel images are worth, and when the actors exist we recreate what was good about the panel with the real actor in place of the placeholder): **the scene craft outlives the actor.** A shot's light, camera, blocking, props and beats are the take's text (sections 2 to 6) and the prompt is derived from them; the face is an input row. So when a placeholder panel is judged, the judgment is recorded against the scene (composition, light, blocking, props; the site's scene-quality field) apart from the likeness field, which for a placeholder is not scored at all. A panel kept for its scene is carried forward as a `design_reference` with the note "scene craft only, identity ignored" and the recreate binds the cast actor as `identity_reference` to the same prompt. Nothing good about a panel is lost to a casting decision, and nothing about the actor in it is ever treated as decided.

## Part 3. Questions for Baldadoon (and Ziggy where marked), not repaired by the writer

Each is one plain question with its lines and the writer's default until it is answered. None blocks the first proof sequence except where noted.

A. **Does the phone really ring?** Nobody but John ever hears the voice in the phone; after 972 the audience knows why. Should the vibration be staged as real (others could notice it), as John's alone, or left deliberately undecidable? Writer's default: undecidable (G2). Touches every call scene; not blocking for scene 3.

B. **Era and phone.** The phone slammed shut (818) and the cell phone of 64 are the only form-factor details the text gives. The flip-phone years with a flip phone, or present day with a smartphone and an anonymous incoming call? Writer's default: period-neutral with the flip phone (G7). Affects the phone insert request (Part 8); the first proof sequence can shoot both inserts.

C. **Rating target.** Silhouette of the organs (682), jars (827), the shower (962), Jill shot (1471), Diego shot in the neck (1759), the boy's shot (36), the raid's two shootings (1827). Implication over display, or as written? Writer's default: implication (G8).

D. **Two slurs** (606, Diego about his own people; 1260, John at Fox). Kept as written, or not? The record keeps them verbatim either way.

E. **Jack's origin.** Why does the second state have a British delivery? The screenplay gives no reason (John raises it at 807; Jack deflects it at 810). Keep it unexplained (the writer's default) or does the author have a reason the film should plant?

F. **Who fires the shot that kills Jill?** Jack says "I'm sick of this" and the gun goes off (1469 to 1471); on the roof Johnny claims her (1590, 1625, 1634). Which state is on camera at 1471? Writer's default, per the roof: the body goes still and silent for the shot, no state speaking, so the roof's claim lands as the explanation. The same question covers the roof itself: after Johnny's "Fuck this!" (1757) the action line has John raise the gun and pull the trigger on Diego (1759); the writer reads it as Johnny's act and plays it the same way, the body firing while no state speaks.

G. **The ammonia.** John tells Jen that Jack said ammonia (154) though Jack never did (114 to 120); Diego confirms ammonia at 222. Intended slip (John knows because he was there) or an oversight? Writer's default: intended, and the film plays it straight with no underline, so it works on a second viewing. Blocking for scene 4 only.

H. **The quality of the cuts.** "Clean cut" (51), "aren't as good as we thought they were" (638), "so much for him not knowing what he was doing" (870). Deliberate (the briefing was wrong) or an oversight? Writer's default: play each as written; no line is changed.

I. **The timeline.** Seven years with Steele (1767); four months earlier (1806); a month before the killings (1803); the boy was fifteen and John is thirty-three (18 years). Consistent as read, but "four months" and "a month before the killings" with five victims across the film's weeks is tight. Does the author want the dates on screen (titles) or kept vague? Writer's default: no date titles.

J. **Johnny's origin, told several ways.** Johnny says he was created the instant the boy fired (1603) and that he took over again at the raid (1895); Steele recounts the boy's cover story that a man named Johnny did it (1773); Fox says the personalities came out only recently and yet that this is why he shot the boyfriend years ago (1770), then that John invented the name Johnny as a cover and the state surfaced months ago (1794). Is the outsiders' account meant to be wrong (the writer's reading: the film's last irony is that the experts have it backwards) or should it match Johnny's? Not blocking; affects the coda's playing.

K. **The agency.** State Police (525, 1809), a PD garage (325), a precinct (516), barracks and a sled (394), a federal case over state lines (379). One agency name for the signage, badges and cars? Writer's default: Massachusetts State Police throughout, since the screenplay names it twice and the others are colloquial.

L. **The witness who saw John leave** (1203 to 1215) is never resolved; the alibi gap (home at 10:00, seen at 8:30 to 9:00) stands. Keep it open as written (the writer's default) or does the author want a beat that answers it?

M. **Props that contradict:** what John tells the agents about the night before (no cigarettes on him, 1165) against what the body did (the butt pocketed at 80, the one lit at 100, the one found at the scene, 1147 to 1156); the gun left on the bed (1508) against the revolver drawn on the roof (1715), which the screenplay covers with "grabs something" from the closet (1508) and the coda's "the gun was never found" (1776). Writer's default: the closet beat is shot so the revolver is planted; the cigarette discrepancy is left exactly as written, since it may be the point.

N. **The ending's tone.** The pinch (1919) and the nurse's shrug end the film on Johnny's amusement. Keep (the writer's default) or end on the open eyes (1877)?

O. **For Ziggy and Baldadoon together: screen length.** The whole screenplay as one film (Part 9 estimates its length) or a contained excerpt first? This is the one decision that sets the size of everything after the first proof sequence.
